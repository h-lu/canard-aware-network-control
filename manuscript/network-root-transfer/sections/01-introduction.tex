\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

In a retarded differential equation, an orbit is represented at each time by
an entire history segment.  This matters near a canard connection: matching
two current states need not match the delayed information that subsequently
returns to the critical direction.  Delayed canards have been established in
several scalar and spatially extended settings
\citep{campbell2009delay,krupa2016complex,krupa2016explosion,
avitabile2020local}, while Lin-type constructions for functional differential
equations provide a natural language for phase-fixed matching
\citep{hale1986heteroclinic,lin1986exponential,hupkes2009lin}.  The question
addressed here is whether a selected complete-history root can be controlled
uniformly after the scalar problem is embedded into finite retarded networks.

Two elementary reductions do not answer this question.  First, copying a
scalar orbit on the synchronous line proves invariance but gives no inverse
for nonsynchronous histories.  Second, row-mass preservation leaves the
collective line invariant, yet delayed coupling layers need not preserve the
transverse complement determined by a stationary measure.  The linearized
network operator is then upper triangular rather than an orthogonal direct
sum.  A current-state projection would remove precisely the delayed
transverse contribution that feeds back into the collective matching row.

These obstructions are distinct from the dimensional-reduction questions
usually organized around spectral modes, synchronization quotients, or
graph limits
\citep{laurence2019spectral,thibeault2020threefold,masuda2022dimension,
ma2023generalized,bramburger2023pattern}.  They also persist in network models
with delayed feedback or canard-type collective dynamics
\citep{balzer2024cascading,liu2026predicting}.  Our estimates instead use
oscillation norms and a common Dobrushin gap; no eigenbasis, reversibility, or
simultaneous diagonalization of the coupling layers is required.

\subsection{Principal result}

The main theorem, \cref{thm:shared-resource}, concerns a shared-resource RFDE
on a finite directed Markov network.  Node curvatures may be heterogeneous,
the delay support is fixed, and the perturbation of each delay atom is
neutral with respect to the stationary row.  After the fold scaling
\(\varepsilon=\delta^2\), a bounded common preparation selects an attracting
and a repelling phase-fixed trace.  Their normalized complete-history gap has,
for every finite network size, exactly one root in a common local parameter
window.  If \(\mu=\delta^2\nu\) and \(\zeta\) denotes the structural
perturbation, then
\begin{equation}
 \mu_{c,N,\mathcal P_N}(\delta,\zeta)
 =\mu_{c,N,\mathcal P_N}(\delta,0)
   +\mathscr C_N\delta^3\zeta
   +O(\delta^4|\zeta|+\delta^3\zeta^2),                 \label{eq:intro-response}
\end{equation}
uniformly in \(N\) and in the declared preparation class, with
\(\inf_N|\mathscr C_N|>0\).  At the root the two selected retained histories
are equal, not merely their present states.  An explicit family with
pairwise-distinct curvatures has the same nonzero coefficient for every
\(N\ge2\) and admits no nontrivial synchrony polydiagonal.

The root in \cref{eq:intro-response} is deliberately indexed by
\(\mathcal P_N\).  The preparation fixes the cutoffs, tail levels, and
phase convention used to select the two one-sided traces.  The theorem proves
that the leading structural coefficient and the displayed remainder bound
are uniform across the declared class; it does not prove that the exact
finite-\(\delta\) root is independent of that selection.  Nor does it
identify the root with an independently selected geometric or physical outer
canard.

\subsection{Mechanism and companion transfer results}

The proof of the main theorem has four steps.  A Dobrushin estimate gives a
dimension-free exponential inverse on the transverse network space.  An
exact fold chart then produces a common contraction for an invariant history
graph, including the required state and parameter jets.  One-sided Gaussian
Green operators solve the trace, phase, and endpoint equations with bounds
uniform in network size.  Only after this range problem is solved do we pair
the reduced field with its adjoint.  Atomwise row neutrality cancels the
direct structural delay term, leaving a mixed-curvature contribution that
produces \(\mathscr C_N\).  Strict monotonicity of the normalized gap then
gives the unique root.  The decisive bridge is the injectivity of the history
graph: equality in the scalar reduced gap becomes equality of the retained
complete RFDE histories.

Three supporting results isolate parts of this mechanism.  For the leaky
FitzHugh--Nagumo network in \cref{eq:leaky-network-v,eq:leaky-network-w},
\cref{thm:transverse} gives dimension- and topology-uniform transverse decay
and a causal complete-line Green bound.  When the endpoint, phase, parameter,
and gap data are collective, \cref{cor:canonical-transfer} uses a bounded
codomain row operation to reduce the upper-triangular Lin operator to the
scalar/transverse direct sum.  Consequently any supplied scalar simple root
has exactly the same root, slope, and orientation in every admitted finite
network.  Finally, \cref{thm:one-gap} records an abstract quantitative
perturbation statement with an explicit root radius and quadratic remainder.

The companion leaky result is a transfer theorem, not an existence theorem
for its scalar root.  The main shared-resource result constructs its own
selected histories and root, but only for a common Dobrushin gap, fixed delay
support, an atomwise row-neutral perturbation direction, and a bounded class
of preparations.  Signed coupling, moving delay atoms, closing mixing gaps,
infinite networks, and preparation-independent canard selection lie outside
the proved scope.

\subsection{Organization}

\Cref{sec:model-results} states the two network classes and the four main
results.  \Cref{sec:network-mechanism} proves first the complete-line inverse
and triangular transfer, and then the invariant-history graph, trace range,
normalized gap expansion, and explicit non-synchrony witness needed for the
principal theorem.  The final section records the exact mathematical boundary
of the resulting root theory.
